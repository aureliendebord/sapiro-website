The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) maintains the Red List, the global reference on extinction risk. In its 2026-1 version, [more than 49,500 species are listed as threatened](https://www.iucnredlist.org/about/background-history), out of more than 175,900 assessed. Here are 24 emblematic cases, from the most critical to the most monitored, with the current IUCN category, the year of the assessment, known population numbers and main causes.

## IUCN threat levels, for orientation

Before the list, a useful reminder. The Red List uses nine categories. Two stand apart (not evaluated, data deficient). The other seven run from least to most critical: least concern, near threatened, vulnerable, endangered, critically endangered, extinct in the wild, extinct. When we talk about "endangered species," we generally mean the three categories the IUCN groups as "threatened": vulnerable, endangered and critically endangered.

A note on method: the IUCN often counts "mature individuals," meaning adults able to reproduce. That figure is always lower than the total population. Both are given below when they exist.

## Critically endangered

**[Javan rhinoceros](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/19495/18493900)**: about 50 individuals according to the [International Rhino Foundation](https://rhinos.org/about-rhinos/rhino-species/javan-rhino/) (page consulted in November 2026), after an estimated high of 76 in 2022 and a wave of poaching. All live in Ujung Kulon National Park in Indonesia. The IUCN (2019 assessment) counts only 18 mature individuals. One site for the whole species: a single disease outbreak or natural disaster could wipe it out.

**[Sumatran rhinoceros](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/6553/18493355)**: fewer than 80 individuals according to the IUCN (2019), 30 of them mature, and 34 to 47 according to the International Rhino Foundation. The population has fallen by at least 80% in thirty years. The IUCN calls it perhaps the most threatened large mammal on Earth.

**[Vaquita (Pacific porpoise)](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/17028/214541137)**: 7 to 10 individuals seen during the [September 2025 survey](https://iucn-csg.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Report-vaquita-acoustic-and-visual-survey-2025-EN-Nov-11-clean-FINAL.pdf), against an estimated 567 in 1997. A species endemic to the northern Gulf of California, killed in gillnets set for illegal totoaba fishing. Calves were still seen in 2025.

**[Cross River gorilla](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/39998/102326240)**: 250 to 300 individuals, fewer than 250 of them mature (2016 assessment), on the border between Nigeria and Cameroon. A subspecies of the western gorilla, split into about ten small isolated groups.

**[Sumatran tiger](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/15966/5334836)**: 400 to 500 individuals according to the last assessment specific to this subspecies, which dates from 2008. The only tiger still present in Indonesia: the Bali tiger disappeared in the 1940s, the Javan tiger in the 1960s.

**[Chinese pangolin](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/12764/168392151)**: no reliable global figure (2019 assessment). For China alone, one study put the population between 25,100 and 49,450 individuals in 2008, after a collapse of 88 to 94% between the 1960s and the 1990s. International trafficking is thought to have involved about 50,000 animals between 2000 and 2013.

**[California condor](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/22697636/181151405)**: 607 individuals at the end of 2025, including 392 in the wild, according to the [U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service](https://www.fws.gov/sites/default/files/documents/2026-02/2025-california-condor-population-status_508-compliant.pdf). Only 22 remained in the wild in 1982, and by 1987 all had been captured for a captive breeding program. The IUCN (2020) keeps it critically endangered: only 93 wild birds had bred successfully at that point.

**[Variable harlequin frog](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/54560/3016252)** (*Atelopus varius*): population unknown (2019 assessment). Once common in Costa Rica and western Panama, it was decimated by the chytrid fungus and was thought to have vanished from Costa Rica by 1996. One population was rediscovered there in 2003, then a few others.

**[Bornean orangutan](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/17975/259043172)**: about 104,700 individuals, against an estimated 288,500 in 1973. The species lost more than 60% of its numbers between 1950 and 2010 (2016 assessment), driven by forest destruction and hunting.

**[Sumatran orangutan](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/121097935/259045437)**: 13,846 individuals (2016 estimate, 2017 assessment).

**[Tapanuli orangutan](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/120588639/259042400)**: fewer than 800 individuals. A species described only in 2017, the least numerous of all great apes.

**[Black rhino](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/6557/152728945)**: 6,788 individuals at the end of 2024 according to the [International Rhino Foundation](https://rhinos.org/about-rhinos/state-of-the-rhino/), up from a low of 2,354 in the mid-1990s. It remains critically endangered because the species lost 85% of its numbers in three generations (2020 assessment).

## Endangered

**[Tiger](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/15955/214862019)**: 3,726 to 5,578 individuals worldwide (2021 assessment), all subspecies combined. The Bengal tiger makes up the largest share: India counted [3,682 tigers in 2022](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleaseIframePage.aspx?PRID=1943922), almost 75% of the world's wild tigers according to the Indian government.

**[Asian elephant](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/7140/45818198)**: 48,323 to 51,680 individuals in 2018 (2019 assessment). The population is thought to have declined by at least 50% in three generations, or 75 years. Fragmented habitat, conflicts with humans.

**[Mountain gorilla](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/39999/176396749)**: 1,063 individuals according to the 2015-2016 (Virunga) and 2018 (Bwindi) surveys, spread across Rwanda, Uganda and DRC. A rare case of a growing population: the IUCN moved it from "critically endangered" to "endangered" in 2018.

**[Whale shark](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/19488/126673248)**: global population unknown, down 50 to 79% over three generations, or 120 years (2025 assessment). The largest fish in the world.

**[Emperor penguin](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/22697752/157658053)**: about 256,500 pairs in 54 colonies. The IUCN moved it from "near threatened" to "endangered" [in April 2026](https://iucn.org/press-release/202604/emperor-penguin-and-antarctic-fur-seal-now-endangered-due-climate-change-iucn): the species lost around 10% of its population between 2009 and 2018, and shrinking sea ice is projected to halve the population by the 2080s.

## Vulnerable: species under watch

**[Giant panda](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/712/121745669)**: 1,864 individuals in the wild according to the Chinese survey of 2011-2014, and 808 in captivity at the end of 2025. Reclassified from "endangered" to "vulnerable" in 2016, with a growing population.

**[Cheetah](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/219/259025524)**: 6,517 mature individuals, or about 7,100 adults and adolescents (2021 assessment). The IUCN projects a 37% decline in fifteen years.

**[Polar bear](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/22823/14871490)**: about 26,000 individuals, with a range of 22,000 to 31,000 (2015 assessment). Trend unknown. The threat comes from melting sea ice.

**[Lion](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/15951/280792135)**: 22,000 to 25,000 adults and subadults in Africa, about 670 in India (2025 assessment). The species now occupies only 6% of its historical range, which shrank by a further 34% in 21 years.

**[Hippopotamus](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/10103/18567364)**: 115,000 to 130,000 individuals, stable population (2016 assessment). Threatened by habitat loss and by hunting, for its meat and for the ivory of its canine teeth.

**[Giraffe](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/9194/136266699)**: 97,562 individuals in 2015, against 151,702 to 163,452 in 1985, a drop of 36 to 40% in thirty years (2016 assessment). The IUCN recognizes nine subspecies, with very different trajectories.

**[Great white shark](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/3855/212629880)**: no global estimate, declining population (2018 assessment). The only figures are regional: about 5,460 individuals in eastern Australasia in 2017, for example.

## Extinct, or nearly so

To give weight to this list, a few species and subspecies that are extinct or on the brink.

The **[Northern white rhino](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/4183/45813838)**: only two females remain, Najin and Fatu, at [Ol Pejeta Conservancy](https://www.olpejetaconservancy.org/2026/04/22/new-embryo-boosts-survival-chances-for-the-northern-white-rhino/) in Kenya. The IUCN lists it as critically endangered, "possibly extinct in the wild". By April 2026, 39 embryos had been produced in the laboratory, with no pregnancy carried to term so far.

The **[West African black rhino](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/39319/45814470)**: no trace since 2006 in Cameroon, listed as extinct by the IUCN in 2011.

The **[Yangtze river dolphin](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/12119/50362206)** (baiji): last confirmed sighting in 2002. It is generally believed to be extinct, but the IUCN keeps it as critically endangered, "possibly extinct".

The **Pyrenean ibex**: [extinct in 2000](https://www.britannica.com/animal/Pyrenean-ibex). A clone was born later, but died shortly after birth.

The **[dodo](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/22690059/261916273)**: last reported in 1662, extinct between 1662 and 1693. A major teaching case.

## The real causes (in order of importance)

Poaching is often named first. The [IPBES global assessment](https://www.ipbes.net/news/Media-Release-Global-Assessment), published in 2019, ranks the five direct drivers in a different order.

**1. Changes in land and sea use.** Deforestation, urbanization, intensive agriculture. The leading cause of nature's decline worldwide.

**2. Direct exploitation of species.** Hunting, fishing, poaching, logging. Decisive for rhinos, pangolins or the vaquita.

**3. Climate change.** Warming, ocean acidification, melting ice. Already decisive for the polar bear and the emperor penguin.

**4. Pollution.** Plastics, pesticides, pharmaceuticals.

**5. Invasive alien species.** Introduced species that outcompete local ones. The bullfrog in Europe, the brown tree snake in Guam, the cane toad in Australia.

## What works

Ecology is not just a story of collapse. A few successes.

The **California condor** (22 wild birds in 1982, 607 at the end of 2025), the **[American bison](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/2815/123789863)** (fewer than 1,000 at the start of the 20th century, about 31,000 in conservation herds today), the **mountain gorilla**, the **[northern bald ibis](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/22697488/130895601)** (numbers rising in Morocco) and the **[Arabian oryx](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/15569/50191626)** (about 1,220 animals in the wild, all descended from reintroductions) show that conservation can reverse the trajectory. But each case takes decades of coordinated effort and massive resources.
