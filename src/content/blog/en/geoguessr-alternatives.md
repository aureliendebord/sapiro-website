The best GeoGuessr alternatives are OpenGuessr and City Guesser if you want to keep guessing a location from what you see, and SAPIRO, Seterra or Worldle if what you really care about is knowing countries, flags and capitals. All of them can be played for free, at least in part, and most run straight in your browser.

If you landed here, the free version of GeoGuessr probably stopped being enough. It is now a very limited offer, and the rest sits behind a subscription. Below are seven options, with what each does well and where it falls short.

## 1. SAPIRO: learn geography instead of guessing it

Full disclosure: SAPIRO is ours, which is why it is first. Second disclosure: it is not a Street View game. If your idea of fun is reading a road sign to work out that you are in Estonia, skip to the next entry.

SAPIRO is a general knowledge quiz. Geography covers all 197 countries with flags, capitals and maps, alongside history, art and nature. What sets it apart from a plain quiz is what happens after you answer: every question comes with an explanation, so you remember why the answer is N'Djamena and not just that it was. There are no ads, including in the free version.

You can [play free in your browser](/en/play/) with nothing to install, or get the app on iOS and Android. The Daily Challenge gives everyone the same set of questions, for free, and you can share your score to challenge a friend. For the head-to-head, see [SAPIRO vs GeoGuessr](/en/blog/sapiro-vs-geoguessr/).

## 2. OpenGuessr: the closest to the original

OpenGuessr keeps the formula that made GeoGuessr popular. You are dropped somewhere in a 360° view, you move around, look for clues, and place a pin on the world map. It runs in the browser and is free to play.

It is the most direct answer to "I want the same thing without paying". You get better the same way, by learning to read landscapes, signs and which side of the road people drive on. It also shares the same limit: the game tells you where you were, not anything about the country.

## 3. City Guesser: the video version

City Guesser swaps panoramas for videos. You watch a walk down a street and guess the city. You hear the language people speak, see the cars, the shop signs, how people dress. It runs in the browser.

Video feels more alive than a still image, and it works well with a few people around one screen. The trade-off is that the game is mostly about cities, so do not expect empty roads in Mongolia.

## 4. Seterra: the map quiz classic

Seterra has been around since 1997 and has stuck to one idea: click on a map to locate a country, a capital, a river or a mountain. The database is huge, available in more than 40 languages, with quizzes for every region of the world.

The free web version is the best way in, and many teachers use it to build their own quizzes. The interface shows its age, and the free mobile version carries ads. For pure map location, nothing beats it. We compared the two in detail in [SAPIRO vs Seterra](/en/blog/sapiro-vs-seterra/).

## 5. Worldle: one country a day

Worldle applies the daily word game recipe to geography. You get a country outline and a handful of guesses, and after each miss the game tells you how far off you are and in which direction. One round a day, in the browser, then you come back tomorrow.

It is short and surprisingly good for learning country shapes. It is not a structured learning tool, and it does not pretend to be.

## 6. StudyGe: an atlas that quizzes you

StudyGe works like an interactive atlas paired with quizzes. You explore the world country by country (population, area, languages), then test what stuck. The map looks modern and the app works offline. Some features are reserved for the paid version, and the content starts repeating after a few weeks.

## 7. Google Earth: wandering without a score

Not a game, but often what people are after when they liked GeoGuessr for the wandering more than the competition. Satellite imagery, 3D views, themed tours, all free and available everywhere. Without a goal, you fly over Patagonia for an hour and remember nothing. Paired with a quiz, it is the opposite: you meet a country, go look at it from above, and it sticks.

## Which one should you pick?

**You want Street View.** Start with OpenGuessr, and try City Guesser if video appeals to you. SAPIRO will not help here. And if you play a lot, a GeoGuessr subscription is still easy to defend: nothing else matches its multiplayer and the number of maps.

**You want to learn flags and capitals.** Guessing locations does not teach that, or only very slowly. Go for a quiz with explanations like SAPIRO, and add Seterra for placing things on a map. Our guides to the [world capitals quiz](/en/blog/world-capitals-quiz/) and [flags that look alike](/en/blog/flags-that-look-alike/) are good places to start.

**You want two minutes a day.** Worldle for the daily outline, SAPIRO's Daily Challenge for a set of questions that is the same for everyone. Both are easy to compare with friends, which helps you keep the habit.

**You are a teacher.** Seterra lets you create your own quizzes and runs in any computer lab browser. SAPIRO suits a no-ads, no-install activity: open the page and the class plays. Google Earth rounds off a lesson by showing what you just talked about.
